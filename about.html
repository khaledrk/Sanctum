ABOUT / PRACTICE

EREÍPIAPÓLIS

EREÍPIAPÓLIS investigates ruinous landscapes and the lives that continue through them.

During the MLA, I worked through repeated site visits, historical research, mapping, sound recording, photography, conversations, observation and design testing.

I want the practice to remain research-led, using selective spatial interventions to document each site, carry its knowledge forward and support new forms of life and use.

THE NAME

EREÍPIAPÓLIS

My interest in polis comes partly from Assiut, my home city, which was known historically as Lycopolis. Discovering that name made me see a place I thought I knew through another ecological and cultural history. EREÍPIAPÓLIS joins ruin with polis: a city whose histories continue through decay, occupation and change.

HERE'S WHAT WE BELIEVE

RUINATION IS A FIELD OF PRACTICE.

These statements are working principles for how EREÍPIAPÓLIS reads, records and intervenes in changing landscapes.

01

Nothing ends where it breaks.

Destruction changes a place and sends its material, memory and use into another phase.

02

Go there more than once.

A site changes between visits. Weather, people, animals, water and materials reveal different things each time.

03

Read the damage.

Cracks, corrosion, rubble, flood marks, missing walls and exposed ground are evidence of active processes.

04

Map what the map misses.

Tracks, sounds, informal routes, occupation, decay, sediment and ecological takeover belong in the drawing.

05

Follow use before intention.

What people and other species actually do with a place matters more than what the original plan expected.

06

Keep the evidence visible.

Design should allow previous lives, conflicts, failures and repairs to remain legible.

07

Work with what is already acting.

Tide, weathering, vegetation, animals, movement and material failure are active forces in the project.

08

Leave room for misuse.

A place can acquire meanings and functions that were never drawn into it.

09

Make space for more than humans.

People share the ground with plants, animals, water, mud, fungi, sediment and changing ecologies.

10

Water has agency.

Flooding, seepage, saturation and tide reorganise access, habitat, material and occupation.

11

Decay can be directed.

Some processes can continue. Some can be accelerated. Some need support, removal or a new structure around them.

12

Restraint is a design act.

Leaving a condition alone can be a deliberate spatial decision.

13

Demolition can prepare another life.

Unsafe or exhausted structures can be opened, dismantled or reworked so that new occupation becomes possible.

14

Every addition will become a ruin.

New work enters the same weather, time and material processes as everything already on site.

15

Draw time.

A landscape project has a past, a present condition and several possible futures. The drawing should make those changes visible.

16

Keep the future unfinished.

Design can establish conditions for change without fixing every later use or outcome.

17

Work with people who know the ground.

Communities, users, researchers, makers and site managers carry knowledge that cannot be produced from the studio alone.

18

Move across scales.

A crack in concrete, a horse track, a marsh, a fort and an estuary can belong to the same spatial argument.

19

Use making to think.

Models, fabrication, drawing and material tests are ways of finding out what a proposal can actually do.

20

Let occupation exceed authorship.

The project gains another life when people and non-humans use it in ways the designer did not predict.

21

Pass the knowledge on.

Mapping, recording, writing, drawing and showing should make the knowledge of a place available to others.

22

Return. Revise. Return again.

Research and design remain accountable to what is actually happening on the ground.
